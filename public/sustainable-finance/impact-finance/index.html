What impact finance adds

Financial return and measurable impact are evaluated together.

The source guide defines impact finance as capital provided with the intention of generating an economic return alongside positive, intentional, measurable and sustainable social or environmental outcomes. It highlights four distinguishing elements.

Intentionality

Impact is part of the investment purpose, not an accidental side effect.

Additionality

The financing contributes to outcomes that would be difficult to achieve without that support.

Measurement

Results are monitored through defined indicators and periodic reporting.

Financial return

The capital is expected to be repaid and to earn a return consistent with the instrument's risk profile.

Impact capital ecosystem

Different funders bring different mandates, tickets and instruments.

Multilateral development banks

Long-term finance, technical assistance and institutional strengthening.

National development banks

Public financing and guarantee programs for strategic sectors.

Impact investment funds

Private capital targeting measurable social/environmental outcomes alongside financial returns.

International cooperation

Concessional resources, technical cooperation and institutional capacity building.

Commercial finance

Banks and financial institutions participating in structured or syndicated financing.

Foundations & institutional investors

Philanthropic, programmatic or private capital with development and impact objectives.

Instrument selection

The financing need should determine the instrument—not the other way around.

Portfolio growth & liquidity

Credit and credit lines are commonly used to expand loan portfolios and manage gradual funding needs.

Capital strengthening

Subordinated debt, equity and quasi-equity may support institutions that need a stronger capital base or greater financial flexibility.

Risk reduction & capabilities

Guarantees, technical assistance and blended-finance structures can support higher-impact or more innovative activities where risk or institutional capacity is a constraint.

Important: the guide emphasizes that there is no universally “best” instrument. Purpose, development stage, capital structure, debt capacity and funder conditions all matter.

The source guide stresses that access is not driven only by the need for capital. It depends on preparation, evaluation and trust-building between the institution and the funder.

Funding readiness

Prepare the evidence before the funder asks for it.

Institutional

History, mission, governance, management team, footprint, products and strategic plan.

Financial

Audited statements, balance sheet, P&L, cash flow, ratios, budget and forecasts.

Loan portfolio

Portfolio size, clients, sectors, geography, delinquency, provisions, write-offs, recoveries and concentration.

Risk management

Credit policy, risk manuals, approval methodology, committees, risk matrix, continuity and internal audit.

Legal & regulatory

Legal existence, bylaws, licenses, ownership, beneficial owners, tax status, litigation and regulatory compliance.

Impact & ESG

Impact objectives, target population, inclusion indicators, measurement methodology, reporting, client protection, gender, labor, ethics, complaints and AML/CFT policies.

What funders tend to evaluate

Solvency alone is not enough.

Financial strength

Portfolio quality, capital structure, profitability, liquidity and capacity to service obligations.

Governance & risk

Management quality, corporate governance, risk systems, compliance and transparency.

Impact evidence

Clear impact strategy, measurable indicators, reporting capacity and environmental/social/governance practices.

Common failure modes from the guide: unclear use of funds, incomplete or inconsistent information, contacting poorly matched funders, underestimating due diligence, weak impact indicators, hiding material risks, slow responses, excluding senior management, focusing only on interest rate, or treating disbursement as the end of the relationship.
